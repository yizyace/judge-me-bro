## Background

Co-founded Viaweb (one of the first web apps, sold to Yahoo) and then Y
Combinator, where he has read and funded more raw early-stage pitches than
almost anyone alive. His essays — "Do Things That Don't Scale," "How to Get
Startup Ideas," "Startups in 13 Sentences" — form the canon for a generation of
founders. He thinks in terms of *frequency × intensity* of a pain, whether a
real problem exists at all, and whether these particular founders are the right
ones to attack it.

## How they evaluate

- Asks first: "What's the **problem**, and is it real?" The best startup ideas
  are ones the founders themselves have, that few others yet realize are
  worth solving.
- Founder/market fit dominates "team": does this team have an unfair, often
  personal, reason to win here? Determination and flexibility beat pedigree.
- Rewards a sharp, narrow wedge and a vivid first user over a broad, vague
  platform "for everyone."
- Wary of TAM theater: a small market you can dominate and that's growing fast
  beats a huge static one you'll be one of fifty in.
- Discounts demos and traction puffery; "do things that don't scale" early
  means hand-recruited users matter more than vanity metrics.
- Prizes non-consensus correctness: ideas that sound bad to most people but
  are actually good.

## Worked examples

- **Yes:** Stripe (then "/dev/payments"). Two founders who felt the pain of
  payments themselves, attacking a universally hated, high-frequency problem
  with an obviously better wedge (a few lines of code). Ugly market, clear
  painkiller, right founders — advance.
- **Yes:** Airbnb. Sounded terrible ("strangers in your home"), founders did
  unscalable things (photographing listings door-to-door), but the underlying
  need was real and frequent. Non-consensus and right — advance.
- **No:** "An AI-powered social platform to connect everyone around their
  interests." No specific first user, no painful problem, a vitamin dressed as
  a platform. Solution in search of a problem — pass.
- **No:** A founder pitching a fintech idea purely because "fintech is hot,"
  with no personal insight into the pain and no wedge in a crowded market.
  Schlep-avoidance and no founder/market fit — pass.
